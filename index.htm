Should you add gold? What history shows

Research illustration only. These results are hypothetical, not personal advice, a recommendation or a forecast. You can lose money.

We looked at holding gold alongside SPY or QQQ, over 2000–2026 and checked back to 1988. The answer depends on one question about you.

Names used here. SPY is a fund that tracks the S&P 500 (about 500 large US companies). QQQ tracks the Nasdaq-100 (about 100 large, tech-heavy companies). BRK.B is Berkshire Hathaway, a single company. “Gold” is a gold fund. “50% / 50%” means half of the money in each, reset to that mix once a year unless stated. “Simulated” means we reshuffled one-month chunks of real history into 100,000 alternative histories; a “poor case” is one where about 1 in 20 of them did worse.

One question helps frame the choice

Could you ever be forced, or tempted, to sell during a market fall?

A. “I can wait it out”

No job-loss risk, no emergencies, and I will not panic and sell.

SPY alone is a reasonable choice, but its result depends on which era you get: typical growth was about 18.6% a year in 1988–99 and 8.4% in 2000–26.

With 40% gold it was about 9.9% and 9.8%, almost level (a feature of these two periods, not a promise); with 30% gold, 12.1% and 9.5%. Both are steadier than SPY alone. As an illustration, $100,000 could grow over 20 years to roughly $506k–$875k with SPY alone (about $506k if it grows like 2000–26, about $875k if it grows like the full 1988–2026 record), or about $646k with 40% gold at either rate. These use past growth rates, not predictions.

Gold’s effect on growth changed sign between eras: it cost growth in the 1990s and added it after 2000. The cushion in scenario B always pointed the same way, but its size varied (for 40% gold, a poor-case fall smaller by 6 points in 1988–99 and 21 points in 2000–26).

B. “Hardship could force me to sell”

A layoff, an emergency, or the urge to sell in a panic, possibly right when markets are down.

If a portfolio were worth $100,000 at its high, a poor-case fall (about 1 in 20 simulated histories were worse) would leave about $36k–$43k with SPY alone, versus about $54k–$59k with 30–40% gold (the range spans two test periods).

In the 2008–09 crisis SPY fell 55% from its high; SPY with 40% gold fell 35%. If you have to sell at the bottom, the loss becomes real. In these tests gold did not stop the fall; it made it smaller, so there was more left to recover with.

Our research view for this case: SPY with 30–40% gold, reset once a year. Lean toward 40% if the cushion matters most (about $57–59k left after a poor-case fall, versus about $54–57k at 30%), and toward 30% if you would rather give up less growth (about 0.5 points a year more typical growth over 1988–2026). The difference is small, so this is a matter of preference, not a tested rule. This is not personal advice.

Bottom line: nobody knows what markets will do next, so this is not a forecast. Gold’s effect on growth depended on the era, but its cushion in a bad fall pointed the same way in every period we tested (though it was smaller in the 1990s). If hardship is possible, 30–40% gold was the cushion in these tests. If you are sure you can wait, SPY alone is reasonable, with a result that swings more from era to era.

Limits behind this answer: the portfolios were chosen after looking at past results; gold before 2004 and QQQ before 1999 are stand-ins, not the funds; costs are included but taxes and inflation are not. See “What could make this wrong” below.

Why we judge by the bad case, not the typical case

  • The typical fall is the middle case. The bad case (about 1 in 20 simulated histories) is the one that is hardest to sit through, and the one that tests whether you can stay invested.
  • Hardship can arrive with bad markets. Recessions tend to bring both job losses and market falls (we did not measure this here), so the need to sell could come at the worst moment. After a 40% loss you need a 67% gain just to get back to the old high.
  • That is why the main test is 2000–26: it contains four real downturns (dot-com, 2008, 2020, 2022). We also checked back to 1988; in 1988–99 gold lost money, which is a different kind of period, to see what changes.

What could make this wrong

  • Gold had an unusually strong run in 2000–26 (typical growth about 10.4% a year, against SPY’s 8.4%). That flatters gold in our main test, which is why we checked back to 1988.
  • Gold is not a perfect cushion. It fell about 29% itself during 2008, so even SPY with 50% gold fell 33% in the financial crisis.
  • The portfolios were chosen after looking at past results, gold before 2004 and QQQ before 1999 are stand-ins rather than the funds, the eleven stocks are survivors that still trade today, and taxes and inflation are not included.
  • The crises above are one path that happened. The next one may look different, and for a 1929-style depression gold could not be tested at all.

Long-history check, 1988–2026

The main test starts in 2000, a period in which gold did unusually well. To see whether the findings depend on that, we repeated the test from January 1988 for SPY and QQQ with gold (same method: reshuffled one-month chunks of history into 100,000 alternative histories, same costs, yearly reset). This is a robustness check, not a replacement for the main test. This check covers the two index funds only; BRK.B and the eleven stocks stay on the main 2000–26 test.

What we found. In the whole 1988–2026 period the poor-case fall was smaller with gold for every mix tested (by 14.3 to 26.0 points), and in 1988–99 alone by 5.2 to 13.9 points. Typical yearly growth with gold, versus the same fund without it, was −3.3 to −1.2 points over the whole 1988–2026 period and −15.8 to −6.5 points in 1988–99, against +1.1 to +1.8 points in 2000–26 (a negative number means gold lowered typical growth). In 1988–99 gold also lowered the typical Sharpe ratio for every mix (−0.42 to −0.08) and poor-case yearly growth by 4.4 to 9.5 points; in 2000–26 the Sharpe ratio rose.

Reading the long history carefully. We re-ran the check with three alternative constructions (index prices for SPY and QQQ, gold from spot prices before 2004, and a rough dividend allowance for QQQ before 1999). The effect of gold changed by at most 0.5 points in growth or poor-case fall and 0.03 in Sharpe ratio. These checks cannot show how a real QQQ fund would have behaved before 1999, since it did not exist. Before March 1999 the QQQ rows use the Nasdaq-100 index price (the fund did not exist and the index price omits dividends, which makes QQQ look somewhat worse than a fund would have been; this favours gold in the QQQ comparison, so we also tested an allowance for it). Before 2004 the gold series is an estimate from spot prices, and before 1993 the SPY rows use the S&P 500 total-return index. These are index stand-ins, not the funds. The long history contains only a few gold regimes, so it adds evidence but does not settle the question.

How to read the numbers

Our main test, W2 (2000–26), includes the dot-com bust, the 2008 financial crisis, the 2020 pandemic shock and the 2022 decline. The shorter W1 test (2021–26) includes 2022, but none of those earlier events. W2 gives us more kinds of past trouble to test against. It does not make an investment safer or rule out a worse future. W1 overlaps W2; it is a recent check, not a second independent test. Before late 2004, the gold part uses an estimate because the gold ETF did not yet exist.

Reading the tables. Monte Carlo (MC): we cut the real daily results into chunks of about one month and reshuffled them into 100,000 alternative histories of the same length. It shows a range of what could happen, not a prediction. P50 is the middle simulated result (“typical”). P05 is a poor-outcome threshold: about 5% of simulated histories were worse, so it is not the worst possible loss. Drawdown (DD) is a fall from a previous high. CAGR is annual compounded growth. SR (Sharpe ratio) compares growth with day-to-day ups and downs; higher is generally better. Δ compares a result with QQQ in the same period (the summary table above also shows the change versus the same fund without gold). The historical backtest is the one path that actually happened and is shown for context only.

Why is the poor case worse than what actually happened? SPY’s actual worst fall in 2000–26 was about 55%, but its simulated poor case is about 64%. Reshuffling real months can line bad months up in an order that never occurred. That is the point of simulating: history is one draw, not the limit of what can happen.

What we cannot know about the future

The future could be very different from the years we studied. Stocks and gold could both fall, and losses could be worse than those shown here. The dollar figures below assume the same growth rate every year. They show how the math works; they do not tell you what your investment will be worth.

Annual reset or buy once and leave it?

What we found: In the long W2 test, resetting once a year had higher middle growth for all nine stock/gold mixes. For the 50/50 mixes, it also had smaller poor-outcome losses. This does not tell us which method will win in the future.

Annual reset restores the chosen mix every January. No reset buys once and lets the mix drift. A calendar reset is not an attempt to predict market turns. The dollar amounts below repeat one growth rate every year; they are examples, not forecasts.

The yearly reset is a trade: in a taxable account it can trigger taxes on gains, which these illustrations ignore. In this historical test, annual reset had higher median growth for all nine mixes. The outcome may differ in the future. No reset had a slightly shallower poor-outcome drawdown for SPY/30% gold and QQQ/30% gold; annual reset had a shallower one for the other seven mixes. At 50% gold, the W2 poor-outcome drawdown was −41.13% with annual reset versus −45.41% with no reset for SPY, −48.69% versus −52.65% for QQQ, and −39.90% versus −42.32% for BRK.B.

Method and limits

Market-asset closes are dividend-reinvested total-return histories. The gold series is a synthetic spot-based proxy before November 18, 2004, then gold ETF total return. The earlier portion was not tradable as that ETF and omits its expense. The mixes in the main MC table and growth charts rebalance at the first January trading close each year and hold shares between resets. The no-reset SPY, QQQ and BRK.B mixes buy once at the first close and then hold the original shares as the weights drift. Single assets remain buy and hold. Every market-asset trade costs 0.05% of the amount traded, including the first purchase. The backtest assumes exact closing-price sizing and fractional shares.

Each MC study resamples 21-trading-day blocks of already completed daily portfolio returns into 100,000 paths. Trading decisions are not recalculated inside simulated paths. Sharpe is calculated from average daily simple return divided by daily return standard deviation, multiplied by √252; historical results use sample standard deviation and MC uses population standard deviation. The risk-free rate is 0%. The portfolios were selected after looking at past results and have no live validation. Future returns, relationships and costs can differ.
